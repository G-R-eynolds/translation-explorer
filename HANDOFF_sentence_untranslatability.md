## 0. Why, and for whom

The user's father (a linguist working on an AHRC bid about translation) responded to the word-level results with three asks:

His running example: *me gusta mucho el duende* → "I really like duende". Here *duende* needs opening up, and so does *gusta*: the thing *pleases me*, and the verb is related to taste ("I really enjoy", "I have a taste for").

## 1. The definition

**The core idea:** untranslatability is a *cost*. It is how much extra target-language text you need before the translation carries the source's meaning. The word-level "how many English words does it take?" curve was a discrete case of this. Here it is generalised.

**Notation:**
- s = (s₁…sₙ) is a source span in language A (a word, phrase, sentence or passage).
- B is the target language (English unless stated).
- t is any candidate B-text, and |t| is its length in tokens of a fixed tokenizer.

**Distortion.** For each meaning space (§2), define:
- **d(s, t) ∈ [0, 1]**, the share of s's meaning that t fails to carry;
- **ℓᵢ(s, t) ∈ [0, 1]**, the same at source-token level, so d is a weighted average of the ℓᵢ.

**Distortion–length curve.**
- **D_s(L) = min { d(s, t) : t ∈ C(s), |t| ≤ L }**, where C(s) is the set of candidate translations generated by the budget ladder (§3).
- Because C(s) is finite, D_s is an *upper bound* on the true minimum (a better translation might exist), and it is non-increasing in L by construction (a lower envelope).
- **Plot it against log L**, so that "twice as long" is the same horizontal step everywhere.

**Untranslatability at tolerance ε.**
- **U_ε(s) = log₂( L*_ε(s) / L₀(s) )**, where L*_ε(s) = min{ L : D_s(L) ≤ ε } and L₀(s) is the length of the standard one-sentence translation (rung B1).
- Read it as: "how many doublings of length you need before the meaning gets across". A span that reaches ε at the standard length scores 0.
- If the ladder never reaches ε, report it as censored ("> log₂(L_max / L₀)"), not as infinity.
- **ε is calibrated, not chosen**: it is the 90th percentile of d(s, B1) over the plain control sentences, i.e. the distortion an easy sentence still has after a normal translation.
- Also report the **area version**, A(s) = mean of D_s over the log-spaced budgets from L₀ to L_max. It is smoother and has no threshold.

**Span version (the heat map).** For a sub-span S ⊂ s:
- d_S(s, t) = Σ_{i∈S} wᵢ ℓᵢ / Σ_{i∈S} wᵢ, and D_{s,S}(L) and U_{ε,S} follow in the same way.
- The weights wᵢ are each token's "information weight" (§2.2), so function words count for little.
- **Non-compositionality:** a sentence's U is *not* the sum of its words' U. *Gustar* consists of simple words and a hard construction. So also report the **construction gap**: U(phrase) − max of U(word) over the words inside it.

**Spread (the second axis).** H_S is the semantic entropy (NLI clusters, as in thread 2) over the English renderings of span S across 20 sampled standard translations.
- A high **leftover** says "write commentary".
- A high **spread** says "show alternatives".

Together the two say *which* of the father's practices fits each part of a sentence.

**What the formalism captures (these become figure captions):**
- **One-to-one substitution** = forcing L ≈ L₀; U is the price of that constraint.
- **Commentary and thick translation** = moving right along the curve.
- **Showing alternatives** = spending budget on spread.
- **Borrowing or coining a term** = enlarging B's vocabulary, which moves the whole curve down. This links to the word-level finding that the gap roughly halves once English has the loan.

## 2. Two meaning spaces

Use **both**, report both, and treat their agreement as part of the result. Neither is privileged.

### 2.1 Concept-layer distortion d_CL (inside the model)

1. Run s, in context, through the model and keep the residual-stream state of each source token at layer ℓ. Do the same for t, in its own context (the target text alone).
2. **Centre per language**: subtract that language's mean state at layer ℓ, computed over its vocabulary pool from `untranslatables/data/pools/` or a sample of sentences. Then L2-normalise.
3. **Semi-relaxed unbalanced optimal transport** between source tokens (mass wᵢ, normalised) and target tokens. Cost Cᵢⱼ = 1 − cos(hᵢ, gⱼ).
   - The **source marginal is penalised** with a KL penalty of strength τ: losing source meaning costs.
   - The **target marginal is free**: extra commentary is allowed and not penalised.
   - Use POT (`ot.unbalanced.sinkhorn_unbalanced` with reg_m = (τ, ∞) or a large value; or `ot.unbalanced.mm_unbalanced`), with entropic regularisation ε_ot ≈ 0.05.
4. **Leftover**: ℓᵢ = 1 − (Σⱼ Pᵢⱼ) / wᵢ, the share of token i's mass left untransported. Then d_CL = Σᵢ wᵢ ℓᵢ. Also record the transport cost ⟨P, C⟩ as a secondary quantity.
5. **Calibrate τ** on the control sentences so that d_CL(control, B1) is low (target ≤ 0.15), while d_CL(s, the B1 translation of a *different* sentence) is high. Fix τ before looking at the test sentences.

**Lessons from the word-level runs (must be applied):**
- At layer 11 of the 3B, representations partly tracked **spelling** (*Brücke* → "brushed"), so don't assume layer 11. **Sweep the layers** and choose by the validation in §6.1. Report the chosen layer plus two neighbours.
- The English "detour" peaked at layers ~18–22 of the 3B. Report d_CL there too: if English translations look closer *only* in the detour layers, that is a measurement of the English-shaped distortion the father is asking about.
- **Spelling check:** rung B2 (keep the source word in the English) will lower d_CL partly because the spelling matches. Compare B2 against B3 (gloss, no source word) and report how much of B2's gain survives.

### 2.2 Round-trip recoverability d_RT (behavioural)

How much of the original can be **recovered from the translation**? Use the same model as a back-translator, teacher-forced on the *original* source:
- **Prompt:** "Translate this English text into ⟨A⟩:\n⟨t⟩\n⟨A⟩:", then score s token by token. Use the same model, and a second model as a check.
- **Information the translation supplies about token i:** Rᵢ(t) = log p(sᵢ | s_<i, t) − log p(sᵢ | s_<i, ∅). Here ∅ is the same prompt with an empty or irrelevant English text: the original's own predictability with no help.
- **Oracle:** Rᵢ* = Rᵢ(t = s), i.e. "Translate this ⟨A⟩ text into ⟨A⟩", the copy task, which is the most a text can supply.
- **Weights and leftover:**
  - wᵢ = max(Rᵢ*, 0) is token i's information weight;
  - ℓᵢ = 1 − clip(Rᵢ(t) / Rᵢ*, 0, 1) for tokens with Rᵢ* > δ (δ ≈ 0.5 nats). Below δ, set ℓᵢ = 0 and drop the token from the weights;
  - d_RT = Σ wᵢ ℓᵢ / Σ wᵢ: **the share of the source's information the translation fails to carry.**
- Aggregate subword tokens to words by summing wᵢ and taking the w-weighted ℓ.
- **Reuse the weights**: use the wᵢ from d_RT as the OT masses in d_CL as well, so both spaces weight content the same way.

**Pitfalls:**
- This is *not* the back-translation JSD of the lattice run. That measure averaged over tokens and diluted one-word meaning flips. Here everything is per source token and only then aggregated, which is the point.
- **Copying:** if t contains the source word (B2) or a cognate, Rᵢ jumps. That is partly legitimate, since borrowing does carry meaning, but it must be separated. Report d_RT with and without source-word tokens masked out of t.
- The back-translator is the same English-leaning model. **Check with a second model**: Aya-23-8B if its licence has been accepted, otherwise Qwen2.5-7B.

## 3. The budget ladder: translational practices as rungs

For each source sentence, generate candidates at each rung. Use the main model, sample 5 per rung at T=0.8 plus one greedy, and write the prompts in English. Record every t and |t|.

| rung | practice (the father's vocabulary) | prompt gist |
|---|---|---|
| B0 | word-for-word | literal gloss, one English word per source word, keep the source word order |
| B1 | standard translation | natural one-sentence translation |
| B2 | keep the word | translate, but keep untranslatable words in the original, in italics |
| B3 | inline gloss | translate, and add a short bracketed gloss (≤8 words) after each hard word or phrase |
| B4 | alternatives | translate, and give 2–3 alternative renderings for each hard part, separated by "/" |
| B5 | footnote | translation plus a footnote of ≤40 words on what is lost |
| B6 | thick translation | translation plus a paragraph of ≤120 words opening up meanings, connotations and grammar |
| B7 | coinage | invent an English term for the hard part, define it in one sentence, and use it in the translation |

The **lengths** from these rungs give the L axis. D_s(L) is the lower envelope over all candidates.

**Targeting test** (cheap; it tests whether the heat map is *useful*). For each test sentence, make two B3 variants of matched total length: one glosses the **top hot spans** from the B1 heat map, the other glosses **random content spans**. Use the greedy B1 translation as the base and write the glosses with the model. If targeted glosses cut d more than random ones, the heat map is telling you where commentary helps. **This is the father's "different translational practice" made operational.**

## 6. Validation (run on the 3B first, before anything is interpreted)

1. **Layer choice for d_CL.** At each layer, compute the AUROC of d_CL separating *matched* control pairs (source, its own B1) from *mismatched* pairs (source, another control's B1). Choose the layer with the highest AUROC, excluding layers where a spelling-only control wins. The spelling-only control: a nonsense English string sharing the source's first syllables.
2. **Ladder sanity.** Mean D_s(L) must decrease from B1 to B6 on the test sentences. If it doesn't, the meaning space is not measuring meaning: stop and report.
3. **Heat-map sanity.** The per-token leftover should be higher on annotated `hot_spans` than on other content tokens (AUROC). Check the father's sentence specifically: *duende* > *gusta* > *mucho*.
4. **Agreement of the two spaces.** Spearman correlation of U and A across sentences, and per-token correlation of ℓᵢ, between d_CL and d_RT. Disagreements go in the report as findings.
5. **English-detour check.** The same d_CL computed at the detour layers vs the chosen layer. Do English translations look systematically closer in the detour layers than in the concept layer? Compare across categories.
